About this page

Kling Shipping is moving container bookings online. When a ship is full, new bookings go onto a waiting list. Just before the container-yard cut-off, operations confirm how much space is left, and the system must decide who on the list sails and who is rolled to next week's ship. Management's one firm principle: being rolled once is acceptable, being rolled twice is a serious service failure.

This page answers the six assignment questions in order, using the two waiting lists in the case: Exhibit 2 (10 TEU free, 5 bookings) and Exhibit 3 (200 TEU free, 22 bookings). Every number was checked by enumeration or solved exactly. The charts are live: switch the controls to compare rules, and press Table on any chart to see the data behind it. The Management deck tab presents the case as fifteen slides, and the Simulation tab tests the two extra steps (cargo class and opt-in splitting) on hundreds of random waiting lists.

How this was made

Six steps, in this order. The model was the easy part; most of the work was deciding what "best" and "fair" should mean.

  1. Frame the request. A rule the system runs on every voyage, not one answer for one ship. VIP customers are served upstream, so the waiting list shares only the leftovers.
  2. Write the three sentences. Objective, decisions and constraints, with the objective as a ranked list because the goals compete, and cargo type as a tie-break.
  3. Build and test rules. Priority-first, utilisation-first and a tiered optimal rule, solved exactly on both exhibits.
  4. Challenge fairness. Customers who chose a voyage lose out to customers rolled into it. We tested native-first and went back to step 3.
  5. Price the trade-offs. TEU left empty, customers rolled, and second rollovers caused, for every rule.
  6. Look beyond the algorithm. Deposits, paid guaranteed space, transparency, and a pilot with KPIs.

What should the rule pursue, and where do fill and fairness conflict?

A question of objectives. It decides what "a better assignment" means before any model is built.

Which plan should Kling pick?

This chart only shows that the clash exists: two plans are Pareto-optimal and no plan does both. Choosing between them, and what to tell the commercial director and Sahel Minerals, is answered in Q3.

Key findings

Rank the goals: never roll a customer twice, then fill the ship. They only clash when space is tight.

  • Objectives, in order: no second rollover → full ship → urgent cargo → booking order → whole consignments.
  • "Better" means winning on the first goal where two plans differ, checked over several voyages.
  • The clash: on SA2612E (Exhibit 2), filling 10 TEU rolls Sahel twice; keeping the promise caps the load at 8 TEU. Q3 picks between them.

A second clash, between customers: those who chose this voyage vs those rolled into it. Rollover priority wins because everyone waits at most once (tested in the recommendation below).

Ranking the goals is the management decision; the solver follows whatever order it is given. Fill and fairness collide only when the free space is small compared with the bookings, as on SA2612E (Exhibit 2), where no plan does both.

Fairness between customers is the harder call. Rolled bookings never take confirmed space; they only order the waiting list. Under rollover priority everyone waits at most once; under native-first some never wait and others wait twice. Customers who chose a voyage get information and choice instead: the real queue at booking, free cancellation, and paid guaranteed space.

How do we write the single-voyage decision as an optimisation model?

A question of formulation. It asks for four things: the decision variables, the capacity constraint, the objective function(s) and the class of problem.

Approach

We model this as multi-objective optimisation. Kling has several goals that compete: never roll a customer a second time, fill the ship, let urgent cargo through, and respect booking order. No single plan is best on all of them, as SA2612E (Exhibit 2) shows.

Underneath, the model is a 0-1 integer linear program: one yes/no variable per booking and one capacity constraint. To handle the competing goals we use lexicographic optimisation: we rank the goals, solve one 0-1 integer program per goal in that order, and lock in each optimum before solving the next. So the problem is multi-objective; the step-by-step part is the method we use to solve it. It is still one decision, who sails on this voyage, made once. The utilisation-only version, with TEU as the single goal, is shown alongside for comparison.

Beyond the case · an extra step we added

We added cargo type as a goal of its own. The case only asks about rollovers and space.

  • Why. A week's delay does not cost every customer the same. Hospital beds, or excavator parts that leave a site idle, hurt far more than furniture. Rollover counts and TEU cannot see that difference.
  • How it is classified. From the declared commodity, checked against the shipping documents, never from a customer saying "urgent": 1 medical, 2 spare parts for machines already in service, 3 everything else.
  • Where it ranks. After TEU, so it only chooses between plans that are equally full. It never costs space and never breaks the no-second-rollover promise.
  • What it changed. Nothing in Exhibits 2 or 3: every medical and spare-parts booking already sails. Across hundreds of simulated waiting lists, though, it changes the plan in most of them. See the Simulation tab.

In Exhibit 3. Class 1: Yamoussoukro Pharma (medical consumables, already rolled twice) and Ivoire Medical (hospital beds). Class 2: San-Pédro Auto Parts (vehicle spare parts, already rolled twice), Korhogo Cotton (cotton gin spares) and Xiamen Heavy (excavator parts). The other 17 bookings are standard.

The downside. Cargo classes only work once Kling has set them up and agreed them: a written definition of each class, agreement from the commercial team, and terms customers accept at booking. Even then the line is partly subjective. Irrigation equipment, generators, telecom equipment and tyres could each be argued into a higher class, so borderline cases need a named owner to decide them, and customers will dispute the result.

Why the goals are ranked in this order

The order is a management choice, not a result of the maths, so each rank needs a reason. The rule of thumb: protect promises first, then avoid waste, then use any free choice well.

  1. Load the waiting list. Both exhibits are in the code; set EXHIBIT to 2 or 3. TEU is worked out from the container counts and checked against the exhibit total.
  2. Build the Q2 model. One binary variable per booking and the capacity constraint.
  3. Solve one stage at a time. Each stage maximises one goal; its optimum is the "achieved" number in the table.
  4. Lock it in. The optimum becomes a constraint, so later stages can never undo it.
  5. Break ties by booking order. Keep each booking, earliest first, if every locked stage can still be met.

C = DATA[EXHIBIT]["C"]  # final space confirmed by operations
W = list(df.index)  # bookings, in booking order
t = df["teu"].to_dict()  # TEU per booking
r = df["rolled"].to_dict()  # times already rolled
k = df["cls"].to_dict()  # cargo class: 1 medical, 2 spare parts, 3 standard


# ---------- 2. The Q2 model ----------
def build_model(locked, fixed_x):
    """One 0-1 ILP: a binary variable per booking, the capacity constraint,
    every stage value locked so far, and any decisions fixed by the tie-break."""
    m = pulp.LpProblem("Kling", pulp.LpMaximize)
    x = {i: m.add_variable(f"x_{i}", cat="Binary") for i in W}  # decision variables
    m += pulp.lpSum(t[i] * x[i] for i in W) <= C  # capacity constraint
    for f, best in locked:
        m += f(x) >= best  # lock earlier stages
    for i, v in fixed_x.items():
        m += x[i] == v
    return m, x


# ---------- 3. Objectives, in priority order ----------
STAGES = [
    (
        "Rolled twice or more, loaded",
        lambda x: pulp.lpSum(x[i] for i in W if r[i] >= 2),
    ),
    ("Rolled once, loaded", lambda x: pulp.lpSum(x[i] for i in W if r[i] == 1)),
    ("TEU loaded", lambda x: pulp.lpSum(t[i] * x[i] for i in W)),
    ("Medical (class 1), loaded", lambda x: pulp.lpSum(x[i] for i in W if k[i] == 1)),
    (
        "Spare parts (class 2), loaded",
        lambda x: pulp.lpSum(x[i] for i in W if k[i] == 2),
    ),
]
SOLVER = pulp.HiGHS(msg=False, gapRel=0)  # gapRel=0: prove the exact optimum

# ---------- 4. Lexicographic optimisation ----------
locked, fixed_x = [], {}
for name, f in STAGES:  # one 0-1 ILP per stage
    m, x = build_model(locked, fixed_x)
    m += f(x)  # this stage's objective
    m.solve(SOLVER)
    best = round(pulp.value(m.objective))  # the "achieved" column
    locked.append((f, best))  # every later stage must keep this
    print(f"{name}: {best}")

# ---------- 5. Booking-order tie-break ----------
for i in W:
    m, x = build_model(locked, {**fixed_x, i: 1})
    stats = m.solve(SOLVER)  # can booking i still sail?
    fixed_x[i] = 1 if stats.status == pulp.LpSolveStatus.Optimal else 0

df["decision"] = df.index.map(lambda i: "load" if fixed_x[i] else "roll")
print(df[["customer", "teu", "rolled", "cls", "decision"]])

Why the business needs this model

Without a model, every voyage is a negotiation. With one, the rule is fast, consistent and priced before anyone approves it.

  • Time. Clerks spend about two days a week arguing about who sails. The model decides in seconds at cut-off, so clerks handle only real exceptions.
  • Trust. Every customer, on every voyage, gets the same published rule, and any decision can be explained in one sentence. That ends the "why them, not me?" calls.
  • Scale. Exhibit 3 alone has 4,194,304 possible plans. By hand, staff fall back on rules of thumb: greedy priority left 10 TEU empty and rolled excavator spare parts.
  • Revenue and customers. It fills space that would otherwise sail empty, and it protects repeat accounts that could book a rival's next sailing.

Why each goal ranks where it does, from the business side

  1. Rolled twice or more · retention. These customers are the most likely to switch carrier. Losing a repeat account costs far more than one voyage's space.
  2. Rolled once · reputation. One more roll breaks the promise management made. A published rule nobody trusts brings the arguments back.
  3. Fill the ship · revenue. An empty TEU is revenue lost for good, so space comes straight after the promises.
  4. Urgent cargo · customer harm. Medical delays and idle machinery hurt most and are the easiest choices to defend in public. Ranked after fill, so it never costs revenue.
  5. Booking order · simplicity. A neutral tie-break that staff can audit and customers accept.

The real benefit: management sees the price of each principle before approving it. Keeping the promise costs 2 TEU on SA2612E (Exhibit 2) and nothing on SA2618E (Exhibit 3).

Answer

One binary variable per booking, one capacity constraint, and either one objective (TEU) or a ranked list of objectives: a 0-1 integer linear program with the structure of a 0-1 knapsack problem.

  • Decision variables: xᵢ ∈ {0, 1}, load or roll. 5 on SA2612E (Exhibit 2), 22 on SA2618E (Exhibit 3).
  • Capacity constraint: Σ tᵢ xᵢ ≤ C, with C = 10 TEU on SA2612E (Exhibit 2) and 200 TEU on SA2618E (Exhibit 3).
  • Class: 0-1 integer linear program (0-1 knapsack); multi-objective once the goals are ranked, solved by lexicographic optimisation.

Why this order: protect the promise first (rolled twice, then once), then avoid waste (fill the ship), then use any free choice well (urgent cargo, then booking order). See the table above.

What class of problem is it? A 0-1 integer linear program with the structure of the 0-1 knapsack problem. Because its goals compete, it is multi-objective, solved by lexicographic optimisation: a sequence of 0-1 integer programs, one per goal.

Why not an LP? An LP decides how much; a 0-1 integer program decides whether. A booking sails whole or not at all, so every variable is binary. Rounding an LP answer can fail, and shadow prices stop being meaningful.

How it extends. Weight or reefer limits would add constraints: a multi-dimensional knapsack. Opt-in splits would turn each variable into a whole-container count: still an integer program.

Key findings

Defend the priority-first outcome: it keeps management's one promise for 2 TEU, and the 2 TEU can be won back.

  • The price of the principle is 2 TEU: 20% of the leftover space but about 0.1% of a 1,700-TEU SA vessel.

To the commercial director: keep the promise; it costs only 2 TEU. Utilisation-first gains those 2 TEU by rolling a repeat industrial shipper a second time. Priority-first keeps the rule credible, and an opt-in split from Atlas or Fujian still fills the ship.

To Sahel Minerals: "Your three containers sail on SA2612E, whole, as booked." You were rolled once because the ship was full, and our published rule puts customers who have already waited first.

To Atlas, Abidjan General and Fujian: "You are first in line on SA2613E." The rule that loaded Sahel today protects you next week, so nobody waits twice. If you cannot wait a week, cancel free of charge.

How should "one postponement is acceptable, two are not" enter the model?

A question of method. It decides whether the promise holds on every voyage or only on easy ones.

Start with the customer, not the model

Before choosing a method, ask what a second rollover means to the person whose cargo is waiting. Shipping is a competitive, repeat business: customers can book elsewhere, they trade price against time every day, and how much a delay hurts depends on the route. These factors decide how firm the rule must be.

FactorWhat it means for the customerWhat it means for the rule
Competition on the laneRival carriers already sail Shanghai and Ningbo to Abidjan every week. A customer rolled twice can book a rival for the next sailing.The real cost of a second rollover is the account, not a week of delay. It has to rank first.
Repeat customersSahel Minerals ships mining parts regularly. Losing it loses every future booking, not just this one.A small penalty undervalues the customer. The promise must hold for everyone, or nobody trusts it.

Key findings

Treat a second rollover as a broken promise and rank it first: on a competitive lane, its real cost is the customer.

  • Priority ordering (lexicographic optimisation) states the promise openly and always gives an answer. Solved properly, it fills all 200 TEU on SA2618E (Exhibit 3).
  • A penalty only protects Sahel when P > 2. The honest P is the lost account, which is large, so it ends up behaving like priority ordering anyway.
  • A hard constraint has no answer on a week when rolled cargo exceeds the space.

When a second roll cannot be avoided: a person offers the customer a choice (compensation, the next Ningbo call or a refund), and voluntary rolls never count toward the limit.

Customers accept waiting when they choose it. Shippers trade price for time every day. A wait the customer chose is a deal; a second wait imposed on them is a broken promise. That is why a flexible fare or a volunteer credit is fine, and a second involuntary roll is not.

The lane is competitive. A customer rolled twice can book a rival's next sailing and take its future bookings too. The true penalty for a second roll is the account, which is why priority ordering, not a small penalty weight, is the right method.

Delay depends on the route. Two rolls on the three-times-weekly SL service cost less time than one on the weekly SA service. As Kling rolls the rule out to other routes, the limit could be set in days late rather than in the number of rolls.

Should bookings be divisible?

A question of policy. It decides how much space packing can recover without hurting the customer.

The trade-off in one sentence

Splitting a booking lets Kling fill the last gap in the ship, but the customer then gets two arrivals, two sets of documents and two delivery dates for one consignment. The question is not whether splitting is efficient, but who should decide when it is worth it.

Proposed policy

Keep bookings whole by default, and let customers opt in to a split that fills the last gap.

  • A flexible flag at booking, with the customer's own minimum part, for example at least 2 containers per shipment.
  • Whole containers only, at most 2 parts per consignment, used only after whole bookings are placed.
  • The remainder keeps its rollover count, so it has priority next voyage. An already-rolled booking is never split unless that customer asks.

Incentive: a small rate discount or priority within the same tier for customers who opt in. The system issues the separate documents automatically.

Splitting only helps when the gap is awkward. On SA2612E (Exhibit 2), whole bookings leave 2 TEU empty; one 40-ft box from Atlas fills it. On long lists like SA2618E (Exhibit 3), whole bookings almost always fill the ship, so splitting would rarely be used. The Simulation tab tests this on hundreds of lists.

The customer should decide. A split costs the customer more than it costs Kling: a second arrival, duplicate paperwork and a consignment that cannot be used until both halves land. Asking at booking turns a forced split into a choice, and a small discount gives customers a reason to say yes.

Protect the customers who already waited. An already-rolled booking is never split unless that customer asks, and any remainder keeps its rollover count, so the no-second-rollover promise still holds for the part that waits.

How will customers behave once the rule is published, and what else should Kling do?

A question of incentives. It decides whether the rule still works after customers learn it.

How customers will game the rule

Once the rule is published, customers will shape their bookings around it. The biggest risk is "rollover farming": booking a ship you know is full so you jump the queue on the next one. That is why priority should only go to cargo that was ready to sail.

  • Paid priority is fair only at one published price, capped, and refunded with compensation if Kling fails to deliver.
  • Paid priority must never mean ranking by deposit size: that turns the queue into an auction that large shippers always win.

The answer

Customers who have already waited go first, longest wait first; then fill the ship; then urgent cargo; then booking order.

  • On SA2618E (Exhibit 3) it loads 200 of 200 TEU, rolls no one a second time and keeps every medical and spare-parts booking.
  • It rolls 4 customers who chose the voyage, all standard cargo, and they are first in line next week.
  • Pilot on the weekly SA service, where a rollover costs most, and review after 8 sailings.

KPIs: second rollovers (target 0), fill rate (≥ 98%), TEU left empty because of priority, medical bookings rolled, native customers rolled, priority-tier size (alert above 50% of space), paid-priority share, clerk overrides.

How we framed it

Every rule is three sentences: what is best, what we decide, what we must respect

Objective

A ranked list of goals

No second rollover → fill the ship → urgent cargo → booking order. Each goal is met as fully as possible before the next is considered.

Decisions

Load or roll each booking

One yes/no decision per booking on the waiting list: it sails on this voyage, or it rolls to the next one.

Constraints

Space and whole bookings

TEU loaded never exceeds the space operations confirm. A booking sails whole, unless its customer agreed to split it.

Ranking the goals is the management decision; the solver does the rest.

Our recommendation

Customers who have already waited go first. Then we fill the ship. Then urgent cargo. Then whoever booked earlier.

One sentence a clerk can say to any customer. Behind it, the system solves the goals one at a time and locks each in before the next (lexicographic optimisation), so a lower goal can never undo a higher one.
